\section{Introduction}
Decision trees are simple to understand and train, but when we use them on high-dimensional data like images with very few training examples, they suffer from high variance. In the Olivetti Faces dataset, each image has 4096 raw pixels, while I only have 6 training images per person. A single decision tree can easily latch onto noise or background pixels and overfit the training images.

To solve this problem, ensemble methods combine multiple models. In this assignment, I study two popular ensemble techniques:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregation)}~\cite{breiman1996bagging}: Here, I train multiple deep decision trees on different bootstrap samples of the training set and average their predictions through majority voting. This helps reduce the variance without increasing bias.
    \item \textbf{Boosting (SAMME AdaBoost)}~\cite{hastie2009samme}: Here, I train shallow trees sequentially. In each round, the algorithm increases the weights of images that were classified incorrectly by earlier trees, forcing the new trees to focus on harder cases.
\end{enumerate}

I built this entire project following all the assignment guidelines. My roll number is \texttt{M26MAC003}, so I use the deterministic seed $26003$ for all random number generators. I implemented both the Bagging and SAMME Boosting algorithms from scratch in Python, relying on scikit-learn only for the individual tree fits. In the sections below, I walk through my baseline single tree, my custom bagging classifier, my boosting implementation, and my measured bias-variance results.

\section{Dataset Protocol and Common Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
The AT\&T Olivetti Faces dataset consists of 400 grayscale images across 40 different people (10 images per person). Each image is $64 \times 64$ pixels. As required, I flattened each image into a 4096-dimensional vector of raw pixel values ($x_i \in [0, 1]^{4096}$) without applying any PCA or feature extraction.

\subsection{Identity-Stratified Split}
For every person in the dataset, I split their 10 images into three sets:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Training Set:} Exactly 6 images per person ($240$ images total).
    \item \textbf{Validation Set:} Exactly 2 images per person ($80$ images total).
    \item \textbf{Clean Test Set:} Exactly 2 images per person ($80$ images total).
\end{itemize}
I generated this split using \texttt{numpy.random.default\_rng(seed=26003)}, saved the split indices into \texttt{data/split\_indices.npz}, and reused this exact partition across all experiments.

\subsection{Masked Test Set for Robustness}
To test how robust my models are to missing facial features, I created a masked copy of the clean test set by setting the central $16 \times 16$ pixel square (rows 24 to 39 and columns 24 to 39) to zero:
\begin{equation}
    x_{\text{test, masked}}[u, v] = 0.0, \quad \forall u, v \in [24, 39].
\end{equation}
This hides key facial parts like the nose and mouth. All my models were trained strictly on clean images and then evaluated on both the clean and masked test sets.

\subsection{OOB Accuracy, Disagreement, and Vote Margin}
For each value of $B$, I tracked three key quantities on the validation set, as plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:q1_dynamics}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Out-of-Bag (OOB) Accuracy:} For each training sample, only the trees that did not see that sample in their bootstrap bag vote on it. OOB accuracy rises from $35.6\%$ at $B=1$ to $80.4\%$ at $B=200$, closely tracking validation accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Pairwise Disagreement:} I measured how often two trees in the ensemble disagree on an image:
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{D} = \frac{2}{B(B-1)} \sum_{i < j} \frac{1}{N_{\text{val}}} \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\text{val}}} \mathbb{I}[h_i(x_k) \neq h_j(x_k)].
    \end{equation}
    The disagreement jumps to $\approx 0.80$ at $B=5$ and stays flat across all $B \le 200$. This confirms that bootstrap sampling keeps the trees diverse.
    \item \textbf{Vote Margin:} The average difference between the winning vote fraction and the runner-up fraction settles around $0.25$ once $B \ge 10$, reflecting consistent voting confidence.
    \item \textbf{Do They Tell the Same Story?} Yes. The individual trees disagree strongly ($\bar{D} \approx 0.80$), but when their votes are averaged, the random errors cancel out, pushing OOB accuracy steadily upward.
\end{itemize}

\section{Multiclass Boosting from Scratch}
\label{sec:q2}

\subsection{SAMME AdaBoost Formulation}
For $K = 40$ classes, I implemented the Multi-class SAMME algorithm~\cite{hastie2009samme} from scratch. Initial sample weights are set uniformly: $w_1(i) = 1/N$. At each round $t$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item I fit weak learner $h_t(x)$ using distribution $w_t$.
    \item I compute weighted error $\epsilon_t = \sum_{i} w_t(i) \mathbb{I}[y_i \neq h_t(x_i)] / \sum_i w_t(i)$.
    \item If $\epsilon_t \ge 1 - 1/K$, $h_t$ is no better than random guessing (0.975 for 40 classes); boosting is safely terminated early.
    \item I compute learner weight:
    \begin{equation}
        \alpha_t = \ln\left(\frac{1 - \epsilon_t}{\epsilon_t}\right) + \ln(K - 1).
    \end{equation}
    \item I update and renormalize sample weights:
    \begin{equation}
        w_{t+1}(i) = \frac{w_t(i) \exp(\alpha_t \mathbb{I}[y_i \neq h_t(x_i)])}{\sum_{j} w_{t+1}(j)}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Ensemble confidence is given by normalized weighted voting:
\begin{equation}
    q_k(x) = \frac{S_k(x)}{\sum_t \alpha_t} = \frac{\sum_t \alpha_t \mathbb{I}[h_t(x) = k]}{\sum_t \alpha_t}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Toy 3-Class Audit}
To verify mathematical correctness, I tested Round 1 on a 3-class, 9-sample synthetic dataset:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Initial weights: $w_1(i) = 1/9 \approx 0.1111$.
    \item Ground truth: $[0,0,0, 1,1,1, 2,2,2]$.
    \item Stump predictions: $[0,0,0, 1,1,1, 1,1,1]$ (class 2 misclassified).
    \item Weighted error: $\epsilon_1 = 3/9 = 0.3333$ (threshold $1 - 1/3 = 0.6667$).
    \item Learner weight: $\alpha_1 = \ln(0.6667 / 0.3333) + \ln(2) = 1.3863$.
    \item Updated weights: Correct samples scaled to $0.0556$, misclassified samples scaled to $0.2222$ ($\sum w_2 = 1.000$).
\end{itemize}
My scratch implementation on faces uses this exact same update rule.

\section{Bias and Variance as Measured Quantities}
\label{sec:q3}

\subsection{Formal Bias-Variance Formulation}
Holding the clean test set fixed ($N=80$), I generated $R = 30$ independent training sets by sampling 6 images with replacement within each of the 40 identities from that identity's original 6 training images. On each replicate $r \in \{1, \dots, R\}$, I refitted the selected Single Tree, Bagging, and Boosting models without hyperparameter modification.

Let $p_r(x_i) \in [0, 1]^K$ denote the predicted class probability vector for test face $x_i$, $\bar{p}(x_i) = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^R p_r(x_i)$ its expectation over resamples, and $e_i \in \{0, 1\}^K$ the one-hot ground truth vector. The measured vector quantities are defined as:
\begin{align}
    \text{Bias}^2 &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \|\bar{p}(x_i) - e_i\|_2^2, \\
    \text{Variance} &= \frac{1}{NR} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{r=1}^R \|p_r(x_i) - \bar{p}(x_i)\|_2^2.
\end{align}

\subsection{Critical Analysis: Is Deepest Always Best for Bagging?}
\paragraph{Finding: No.}
Conventional machine learning lore suggests that bagging performs best with maximally deep, unconstrained trees because bagging exclusively reduces variance without altering bias. Under this logic, one should grow trees to absolute purity ($\text{depth}=\text{None}, \text{leaf}=1$).
However, my empirical data refutes this claim:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Comparing the \textbf{Selected} depth ($\text{leaf}=2$) against the \textbf{Deep} depth ($\text{leaf}=1$), the unconstrained deep tree increases base tree variance from $0.4912$ to $0.5420$ with negligible reduction in bias ($0.8124 \to 0.7915$).
    \item When bagged ($B=100$), the ensemble test accuracy improvement is actually \textbf{larger} for the regularized tree ($+35.8\%$) than for the maximally deep unconstrained tree ($+34.5\%$).
    \item \textbf{Leaf Occupancy and Disagreement Explanation:} As established in Section~\ref{sec:q1}, unconstrained trees generate singleton leaves ($9.09\%$) that split on sensor noise in raw pixels. These pure-noise splits create pairwise tree disagreements on spurious features rather than semantic face geometry. Bagging averages these errors, but regularizing $\text{min\_samples\_leaf}=2$ ensures that every leaf represents a true sub-cluster of faces, enabling bagging to achieve higher fidelity and lower total risk.
\end{itemize}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Most Stable Model:} Bagging ($B=25$) is decisively the most stable model, exhibiting both dramatic variance reduction across training resamples ($0.6266 \to 0.0481$, a \textbf{92.3\%} drop) and high resilience under structural occlusion ($78.75\% \to 55.00\%$).
    \item \textbf{Most Sensitive to Missing Region:} The Single Tree is catastrophically vulnerable to missing regions, plunging from $50.0\%$ to $5.0\%$ (near random guess $1/40 = 2.5\%$). Its greedy coordinate splits fail when the central $16 \times 16$ facial patch is zeroed out.
    \item \textbf{Is Lowest Variance Necessarily the Best Model?} No. SAMME Boosting achieves the lowest variance of all three models ($0.0187$), yet its test accuracy is poor ($43.75\%$ clean, $32.50\%$ masked) because its squared bias remains stubbornly elevated ($0.8798$). \section{Conclusion}
In this assignment, I demonstrated the real-world differences between Bagging and Boosting on raw image data. Bagging works wonderfully because diverse, low-bias decision trees cancel out each other's variance errors while retaining facial recognition capabilities under occlusion. In contrast, SAMME AdaBoost struggles because it repeatedly inflates the weights of hard, noisy images, trapping shallow trees in repetitive errors. Finally, my bias-variance measurements prove that light regularization ($\text{min\_samples\_leaf}=2$) beats unconstrained deep trees by eliminating noise memorization.
